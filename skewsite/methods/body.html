How skew is measured: metrics and interdependencies in a paper and in its blog

Overview

This appendix explains a tool for comparing the technical content of a scientific paper with that of a firm blog post discussing it. The tool extracts two components from each document: (i) metrics, the dimensions a text treats as measurable, and (ii) interdependencies, the relationships it establishes between those metrics. It then maps the blog’s metrics and interdependencies onto that of the paper. The comparison shows which technical dimensions the blog emphasizes, omits, or introduces relative to the paper.

For example, qubit count, circuit depth, and fidelity are metrics. “Fidelity falls as qubit count and circuit depth grow” states interdependencies between fidelity and qubit count, and between fidelity and circuit depth. A text may simply report both depth and fidelity, failing to state the interdependence between them; or report only fidelity, omitting qubit count and circuit depth as well as their interdependencies. Accounting for each of these metrics and interdependencies can enable a comprehensive analysis of what a blog keeps or discards relative to its underlying paper.

Source Metrics Interdependencies
Paper Defined mathematical variables and measurable dimensions named in prose Relationships established by equations, explicit prose statements, and stated conditions
Blog Measurable dimensions identified in the blog’s wording or matched to the paper’s terminology Relationships stated within a sentence through an explicit connective, together with stated conditions

Table A1 summarizes how metrics and interdependencies are extracted from the papers and blogs. Each document is initially coded without access to the other document, with metrics identified before interdependencies. A subsequent mapping stage compares the two documents. Section 2 explains the extraction rules, Section 3 provides a worked example, Section 4 combines the two documents in a map, and Section 5 explains how the comparison measures can inform organizational research.

Measurement

Metrics in a paper

The tool reads the paper’s main text, including its abstract, introduction, preliminaries, results, and methods; appendices and supplementary material are excluded from the intended coding scope. It reconstructs the paper from its source, such as a PDF or LaTeX file, and presents it in HTML. Selecting a metric displays the relevant sentences and equations, allowing a reader to verify what was extracted and where it occurs. Errors in text reconstruction or equation cropping can affect extraction, so these passages remain available for inspection.

Metrics identified from mathematical symbols

Mathematical notation provides the first basis for identifying paper metrics. A variable defined in the paper is recorded together with any associated definition. For example, “n is the number of qubits” identifies qubit count, “t is the simulation time” identifies simulation time, and a definition of FXEB as cross-entropy benchmarking fidelity identifies that fidelity measure. The paper’s own definitions anchor these decisions rather than requiring an LLM to infer the dimension from a general discussion of the technology, allowing us to identify metrics as objectively as possible.

Dummy indices, such as i in the sum ∑i=1nxi\sum_{i\ = \ 1}^{n}x_{i}, are excluded. Objects used only as operators, matrices, or transformations are also excluded. For example, a Hamiltonian H describes how a quantum system evolves. H is excluded when used only in this mathematical role. The measurable quantities that enter the Hamiltonian can nevertheless enter the inventory of metrics.

Metrics identified from prose

The tool also uses a large language model to identify measurable dimensions named in prose, to extend the reach of the tool beyond what metrics have been formally notated in a mathematical symbol or equation. Examples include gate complexity, classical computing cost, and quantum volume. The model reads the noun phrase in context and identifies what can be counted, timed, compared, or otherwise measured. Expressions such as “the number of qubits,” “low error rate,” and “cost grows with circuit depth” make the relevant dimensions explicit in different ways. The tool also records a setting when the paper explicitly states a result under it. A setting is the model, method, platform, or regime to which a statement applies. For example, “for a sequential implementation” identifies the named method, sequential implementation, while “for a low-rank Hamiltonian” identifies rank, the property described as low. These settings are treated as metrics because they distinguish the circumstances under which a technical relationship holds. They need not be numerical quantities; the tool’s metric inventory therefore includes both measured dimensions and explicitly named conditions of applicability.

Names and aliases

A paper may use several names for one metric. The tool records these names in an alias table, with a quotation for each. For example, suppose a paper writes, “We denote gate count by g and refer to it as gate complexity.” The symbol g, “gate count,” and “gate complexity” are then aliases of one metric because the paper explicitly identifies them as such. The record also identifies where the metric is first named, such as the abstract or a numbered equation.

The tool keeps distinct wordings separate unless they are mechanical variants or the paper explicitly joins their names. Singular and plural forms, capitalization, and hyphenation do not create new metrics. Nor does the symbol n create a second metric when the paper defines it as “number of qubits.” However, “gate error rate,” “error rate,” and “fidelity” remain separate unless a naming statement equates them. Note that a sentence saying that fidelity depends on error rate establishes an interdependence; it does not establish that they are alternative names for one metric. This distinction is critical to prevent metric names from conflating with the interdependencies between them (which are measured later).

The metric boundary slider

The eventual mapping between a blog’s and a paper’s metrics and interdependencies can be very sensitive to how the metrics are defined in the first place. The tool then allows the user to define the semantic boundary between metrics. All metrics are recorded in the database at their lowest level of granularity, to preserve as much heterogeneity as possible. This inventory contains the separately identified metrics, their names, and the supporting quotations. The user can then adjust a metric boundary slider to determine the extent to which different terms are conflated. At the fine end, distinct coded terms remain separate. Moving toward coarse combines terms whose meanings are sufficiently similar. A reader can therefore examine whether a result depends on treating “error rate” and “gate error rate,” for example, as separate dimensions or as one.

Similarity is assessed from a short definition of each metric: what it measures, what it is a quantity of, and its unit when stated. For example, “the difficulty of finding the most likely bitstrings on a classical computer” may describe a dimension similar to the paper’s “classical computing cost” despite sharing few words. Definitions help make that comparison.

The tool converts the definitions into numerical representations with the sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2 model and compares them using cosine similarity. Terms combine when their similarity reaches the chosen threshold. Combinations can form chains: if A is similar enough to B, and B to C, all three combine. Paper metrics represented by mathematical symbols are kept separate by this slider; a symbol joins its defined phrase through the naming rule above.

Adjusting the slider recomputes the displayed metrics, interdependencies, and comparison measures without changing the underlying coding. The slider setting therefore forms part of any reported result. Section 4 explains how the same setting affects matching between the paper and the blog. The worked example uses the fine setting so that each named metric remains separate.

Interdependencies in a paper

The tool first identifies paper interdependencies from the mathematical relationships stated in equations. As with metrics, the paper’s formal notation provides a basis for identifying them as objectively as possible. It then uses prose to extend coverage to relationships expressed without an equation. The tool retains the source of each interdependence and the conditions under which it is stated to hold.

Interdependencies established by equations

Interdependencies between metrics are elicited through their mathematical relationships in the paper’s numbered equations. For example, V = IR relates voltage V, current I, and resistance R. Only relationships stated in the paper enter the calculation. The tool does not add an unstated physical law: if the paper states V = IR but never states P = IV, the tool does not introduce the latter relationship between power, current, and voltage from outside knowledge.

One way to extract interdependencies is to count which metrics occur together in an equation. This makes the result sensitive to how an author presents mathematically equivalent information. Suppose a paper states:

V=IR,P=IVV\ = \ IR\ ,\ \ P\ = \ IV

Substituting the first equation into the second gives P = I²R. Printing this additional equation makes power and resistance appear together for the first time. A rule based on cooccurrence would therefore add an interdependence, although the equation supplies no new mathematical information. The relationship between power and resistance is already implied by the first two equations.

We therefore need to undertake an algebraic analysis to remove redundant equations and identify interdependencies implied by the equations jointly. This allows the tool to recover the same relationships whether an author prints an algebraic consequence separately or leaves it implicit.

Removing redundancy and identifying implied interdependencies

For polynomial equations, the tool uses a Gröbner basis (Cox, Little, and O’Shea 2015), a standard form of the equation system that permits systematic checks of whether an additional equation follows from it. An equation already obtainable by rearranging, substituting, or eliminating variables adds no independent mathematical relationship. For example, V = IR and P = IV already imply P = I²R. Reprinting that third equation adds another source passage but no independent relationship. The tool also reports the number of independent mathematical relationships, termed equation-system rank.

After testing the equations for redundancy, the algebraic analysis identifies minimal groups of interdependent metrics. A group is interdependent when specifying all but one member restricts the remaining member to finitely many possible values, at a typical solution of the equations. It is minimal when no smaller subset already has this property. For example, {V, I, R} is such a group under V = IR. The definition permits several possible values: under P = I²R, knowing P and R may leave both a positive and a negative value for I. The two values still reflect a constraint on I.

Two paper metrics are counted as interdependent when they belong to a common minimal group. The size of the smallest group containing a pair records its directness: a group of three requires one further metric to constrain the pair, whereas a group of four requires two. Thus, a smaller group means that fewer other metrics must be known to relate the pair. Through this treatment, the tool can then extract not only the existence of interdependence between metrics, but also the degree of interdependence.

Inequalities, asymptotic bounds, expectation expressions, and operator statements cannot be handled by this algebraic treatment of scalar equalities. They are therefore interpreted by the language model. For each statement, it identifies which pairs of metrics the statement makes interdependent and retains the equation reference and its interpretation. An error bound, for example, can relate computational cost to target accuracy without specifying an equality between them. These model-coded interdependencies remain distinguishable from those calculated from equalities; they receive no algebraic group size.

Interdependencies stated in prose

As with metrics identified from prose, this step extends the tool’s reach beyond relationships expressed in mathematical notation. A prose interdependence requires two coded metrics and an explicit connective in one sentence. “Fidelity falls as circuit depth grows” qualifies because it states how one metric varies with the other. “We report fidelity and circuit depth” does not. Connectives include depends on, scales with, limited by, requires, and determines. The tool retains the sentence, the connective, and the two metrics, allowing the reader to assess the interpretation.

A statement denying dependence is retained but does not itself contribute an interdependence. For example, “cost does not scale with the number of terms” records independence between those metrics under the stated circumstances. An inverse relationship, such as fidelity falling as depth increases, still counts as an asserted interdependence. Thus, the distinction is between asserting and denying a relationship, rather than between a positive and negative slope.

Conditions on an interdependence

Both equations and prose can establish a relationship that holds only under specified circumstances. These circumstances are its conditions: for example, a relationship may apply only at certain temperatures or under a particular implementation method. The condition can appear in the sentence itself, the assumptions of a theorem, an equation’s case split, or an explicitly stated section setting. Its metrics are identified under the rules in Section 2i; the task here is to connect them to the relationship they qualify. The condition is recorded with its supporting words and location. Table A4 illustrates these sources and the interdependencies they add.

If the relationship between metrics A and B depends on a condition naming metric C, the tool also records interdependencies A–C and B–C. The same rule applies when the conditioned statement denies an A–B relationship. For example, a statement that cost does not scale with the number of terms under a low-rank condition makes rank relevant to interpreting both cost and the number of terms. When two metrics are named in the condition, each is associated with A and B; their presence in the same condition does not by itself establish an interdependence between them.

The same pair can be interdependent under one condition and independent under another. The tool retains both statements. Its overall pair comparison counts an interdependence if the paper establishes it under at least one recorded condition; the accompanying evidence identifies that condition.

Metrics in a blog

Blog metrics are identified in the same way as metrics extracted from a paper’s prose, using the measurement frames and naming rules in Section 2i. The blog is initially coded on its own, so a metric can be identified even if the paper does not discuss it.

The tool then maps blog metrics to potential paper counterparts by checking the blog against the paper’s alias table. If the paper calls n “number of qubits,” a blog’s “53 qubits” can be associated with that metric. Each candidate match is checked in context: “time” in “at the same time” is not a mention of simulation time. A valid match can identify an existing blog metric, add a further mention of it, or recover a metric missed in the initial blog pass.

The same metric boundary slider detailed above also controls the granularity of this mapping. At fine, a match requires an exact match to one of the paper’s documented names. Moving toward coarse can match an otherwise unmatched blog metric to its most similar paper metric when their definitions reach the selected threshold. The slider therefore governs both which terms are combined within a document and which differently worded metrics can be matched across the two documents.

Interdependencies in a blog

The tool identifies blog interdependencies using the same rule for interdependencies expressed in paper prose (Section 2ii): one sentence must connect two coded metrics through an explicit connective.

Worked example

A short paper on electricity

Consider an illustrative paper whose abstract reads: “We study voltage V, current I, resistance R, power P, charge Q, and elapsed time t.” It states four equations:

(1)V=IR(1)\ \ V\ = \ IR

(2)P=IV(2)\ \ P\ = \ IV

(3)P=I2R(3)\ \ P\ = \ I^{2}R

(4)Q=It(4)\ \ Q\ = \ It

It also states: “At temperatures T below 4 kelvin, voltage V is independent of current I.” The resulting inventory contains seven metrics: V, I, R, P, Q, t, and T. The temperature threshold is a value of T, rather than a separate metric.

Equation (3) follows from substituting equation (1) into equation (2). It therefore adds no independent mathematical relationship. Equations (1), (2), and (4) supply three independent relationships and establish the eight minimal groups in Table A5.

Table A5. Minimal interdependent groups in the electricity paper
Metrics in the group Relationship Group size Printed in the paper
{V, I, R} V = IR 3 Equation (1)
{V, I, P} P = IV 3 Equation (2)
{I, R, P} P = I²R 3 Equation (3)
{V, R, P} V² = PR 3 No
{I, Q, t} Q = It 3 Equation (4)
{V, R, Q, t} Vt = QR 4 No
{V, P, Q, t} QV = Pt 4 No
{R, P, Q, t} Q²R = Pt² 4 No

Four of the relationships are implied without being printed separately. Eliminating I between V = IR and P = IV gives V² = PR; eliminating I between V = IR and Q = It gives Vt = QR. These groups count because they follow from the paper’s equations. By contrast, {V, I, R, Q} is not minimal: its subset {V, I, R} already forms an interdependent group.

Every pair among V, I, R, P, Q, and t belongs to at least one minimal group, giving fifteen interdependencies. Their directness varies. Voltage and resistance share the three-metric group {V, I, R}, so fixing current restricts their relationship. Voltage and time require a four-metric group, such as {V, R, Q, t}; fixing resistance and charge restricts their relationship.

The temperature sentence records independence of voltage and current in its stated regime without erasing the relationship established elsewhere. Because the condition names T, it adds V–T and I–T. The paper therefore has seventeen distinct interdependencies: six each for voltage and current, five each for resistance, power, charge, and time, and two for temperature.

A short blog post about the electricity paper

Suppose a blog post contains three sentences:

1. “Our new paper shows that the power a device draws rises with the current through it.”

2. “The power a device can safely draw decreases as its temperature increases.”

3. “The paper also reports how much charge each device delivered.”

The first two sentences identify power, current, and temperature through scaling expressions. The third identifies charge through “how much charge.” The blog thus contains four metrics and five metric mentions: two for power and one each for current, temperature, and charge. All four names match the paper’s terminology, giving counterparts P, I, T, and Q.

The first sentence states current → power, retaining the paper’s I–P interdependence. The second states temperature → power, an interdependence absent from the paper. The third names charge without relating it to another metric. Voltage, resistance, and time are absent from the blog.

Putting both together checking the blog against the paper

The paper and blog representations are combined in a map. Each blog metric points to the paper metric or metrics it represents under the mapping rules in Section 2iii. This set is its preimage. In the electricity example, the preimages of current, power, temperature, and charge are {I}, {P}, {T}, and {Q}. An unmatched blog metric has an empty preimage. Read in the other direction, the corresponding blog metric is the paper metric’s blog image. Voltage V, resistance R, and elapsed time t have no blog image in this example.

For each pair of paper metrics, the map records three things: whether the blog names both metrics, whether the blog states an interdependence between them, and whether the paper establishes that interdependence. These answers distinguish a retained interdependence from one omitted with both metrics named, one metric named, or neither metric named. Blog interdependencies absent from the paper, and those involving an unmatched metric, are recorded separately.

In the worked example, current–power retains the paper’s I–P interdependence. I–Q is omitted although both metrics are named; Q–t has only one metric named; V–R has neither named. Temperature–power is an additional blog interdependence relative to the paper. Retention concerns the presence of interdependence between the corresponding metrics. It does not require identical direction, magnitude, functional form, or conditions; those details remain available in the source passages.

Measures derived from the map

The mapping in Section 2iii permits many possible comparisons between paper and blog, and even between papers. The metrics and interdependencies essentially produce different conceptual networks, which can be compared with a variety of network algorithms.

The tool offers two basic ways of interpreting the differences between the paper’s and blog’s conceptual networks: where a paper’s metrics and interdependencies may be under or over represented in its companion blog.

Metric salience

Table 1 in the tool compares the share of attention given to each paper metric in the two documents. AA is its share of paper metric mentions. Each mention is a distinct sentence using one of the metric’s names, or a numbered equation containing it.

Table A6 reports the worked example using the metric-salience measures shown in Table 1 on the website.

BB is the share of blog metric mentions assigned to the corresponding paper metric. Its denominator includes all blog metrics, including unmatched ones; each metric counts at most once per sentence. Mention skew compares the paper and blog shares:

Mentionskew=BAMention\ skew\ = \ \frac{B}{A}

A ratio above one indicates a larger share of attention in the blog; below one indicates a smaller share. The electricity paper has 21 metric mentions: six in the abstract, twelve across its four equations, and three in the temperature sentence. Power appears in the abstract and equations (2) and (3), giving A = 3/21. It receives two of the blog’s five mentions, giving B = 2/5. Its mention skew is therefore (2/5)/(3/21) = 2.80.

The ratios use the website’s symbols and shading: ▲ for overrepresentation (at least ×1.41), ● for approximate proportionality (above ×0.71 and below ×1.41), and ▼ for underrepresentation (at most ×0.71). Darker fills identify ratios of at least ×2 or at most ×0.5.

The tool also compares blog attention with a metric’s centrality in the paper’s network of interdependencies. C is PageRank, calculated on the undirected paper network with damping 0.85 and normalized to sum to one. Centrality increases when a metric is interdependent with many metrics, or with metrics that are themselves central. Centrality skew, B/C, compares the blog’s attention with this structural position rather than with the metric’s frequency of mention.

An unmatched blog metric contributes to total blog attention but has no paper-relative ratio. When one blog metric represents several paper metrics, its mentions count toward each without dividing the credit; the mapped B shares may therefore sum to more than one. If A is zero and B is positive, mention skew is infinite; if both are zero, it is undefined.

A metric with no paper interdependencies has no defined share supported. If the blog retains none of the paper’s interdependencies, selectivity is undefined. D includes partners absent from the blog, so the measure reflects both omission of partner metrics and omission of relationships between named metrics.

Conclusion

These measures allow organizational research to examine how firms select and organize technical information. A blog may emphasize fidelity while omitting the circuit depth, computational cost, or operating conditions related to it in the paper. Metric salience captures the allocation of attention; interdependence salience captures the retention of those relationships. The underlying passages permit further assessment of how the firm represents their direction, magnitude, and conditions.

Researchers can also use network measures to examine whether communication concentrates on a few central metrics, preserves clusters of interdependent metrics, or separates dimensions that the paper relates. These conceptual networks can be compared across a firm’s other artifacts, such as patents and press releases, across time, or across firms.
